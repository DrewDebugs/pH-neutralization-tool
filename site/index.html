Why is pH control so hard?

This is an AI project: an AI wrote the code, and my job was to understand, test and defend every equation and every important line. The result is a practical tool for an engineer designing or troubleshooting a wastewater neutralization system. It shows why pH is hard to control (the process gain jumps by orders of magnitude near pH 7) and how engineers cope: staged tanks, tank sizing, reagent strength and tuning.

What the tool does

Equilibrium from a charge balance

HCl, optional acetic acid and NaOH at 25 °C. pH is found by bisection on a function that always has exactly one root.

Titration curve & process gain

See the steep cliff at equivalence and the gain d(pH)/d(reagent) on a log scale, with buffering by a weak acid.

1–3 stirred tanks in series

Each stage has its own NaOH valve and PI loop, with valve limits, anti-windup, probe lag and dead time.

Disturbance response

Step the influent flow or acid strength and watch the true and measured pH, and the valve hit its limits.

Routh–Hurwitz stability

Linearize, get Gp(s) = Kp/(τs + 1), and see the stable Kc range collapse near pH 7.

Staging design helper

Compare 1-, 2- and 3-stage designs by the valve rangeability and resolution each stage actually needs.

How it works

Track what reactions can't change

Total chloride, sodium and acetate are balanced like a mixing problem. No reaction terms, no stiffness.

Solve for pH algebraically

Electroneutrality turns the three totals into a pH at every time step.

Linearize and apply Routh

The tank gain is the titration slope, so the stability limit is (lags) ÷ (chemistry).

Design around it

Stage the neutralization so each valve works on a part of the curve it can actually control.
