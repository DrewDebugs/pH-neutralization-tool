The control loop, one stage

Only the tank block depends on the chemistry: its gain Kp is the slope of the titration curve and changes by orders of magnitude with the set point. Every other block is a fixed lag.

State variables

Reaction invariants

Per tank: a = total chloride, b = total sodium, w = total acetate (mol/L). Neutralization moves H+ around but can't create or destroy these, so their balances are pure mixing balances. pH is calculated from them, not integrated, which removes the stiffness an [H+] balance would have near pH 7.

Modeling choice

Why a valve lag?

A tank (1st order) plus a probe (1st order) under P-only control is a 2nd-order loop: stable for every Kc > 0, so Routh gives no upper limit. Real loops always have a third lag or dead time. Set τv = θ = 0 in the tool and the limit becomes ∞.

Key result

Lags ÷ chemistry

Gp(s) = Kp/(τs + 1) with τ = V/Q, and Kp is the titration slope. The Routh limit Kc,u = (a1a2/a3 − 1)/Kp shrinks exactly as fast as the slope grows, while Pu = 2π√(a3/a1) doesn't change at all.

Assumptions
  1. 25 °C, ideal solutions (activity coefficients = 1). Kw = 1.0×10−14; acetic acid Ka = 1.75×10−5 (pKa 4.757).
  2. HCl and NaOH fully dissociated; acid–base reactions are instantaneous (equilibrium at every instant). Only mixing is dynamic.
  3. Each tank is a perfectly mixed CSTR with constant volume (overflow); volumes are additive, so outlet flow = inlet flow + reagent flow.
  4. Influent contains no sodium. Reagent is an NaOH solution (no heat of dilution).
  5. pH probe = first-order lag τm; transport dead time θ in the measurement path (rounded to whole time steps).
  6. Valve: delivered flow follows the controller output through a first-order lag τv, limited to [0, FR,max]; linear characteristic, no stiction.
  7. No CO2 exchange, no precipitation, no temperature effects.
